# Warren Buffett — Investment Framework

## Core philosophy

Buy **wonderful businesses at fair prices** and hold almost forever. Money is made from **compounding owner earnings** inside businesses with durable competitive advantages — not from trading or macro timing.

## How he chooses stocks

### Step 1 — Circle of competence
Only analyze businesses you can explain to a smart 12-year-old. Skip if economics are opaque (many tech shifts, complex derivatives banks).

### Step 2 — Economic moat (durability test)
| Moat type | Examples | Test question |
|-----------|----------|---------------|
| Brand / pricing power | Consumer staples, insurers with trust | Can they raise prices without losing share? |
| Cost advantage | Scale logistics, low-cost producer | Can competitor replicate at same unit cost? |
| Network effects | Payments, exchanges | Does product get better with users? |
| Switching costs | Enterprise software, banking relationships | Painful to leave? |
| Regulatory license | Utilities, narrow banking | Limited competition by law? |

**Reject** commodity businesses: airlines, pure metals, undifferentiated manufacturing unless price is absurd.

### Step 3 — Financial filters
- **ROIC** consistently above cost of capital (often >12–15% through cycles)
- **Owner earnings** ≈ net income + D&A − maintenance capex (not growth capex)
- **Debt:** prefers low leverage; hates equity holders subsidizing bad capital allocation
- **Reinvestment runway:** can retain earnings at high ROIC for decades

### Step 4 — Management
- Rational capital allocation (buybacks when cheap, not empire building)
- Honesty in letters; no promotional accounting
- Treats shareholders as partners

### Step 5 — Valuation
- **Intrinsic value** = discounted owner earnings (conceptually; he uses simplified heuristics)
- **Margin of safety** at purchase — but pays up for quality (See’s Candies lesson)
- Compare to **risk-free rate** (T-bills / G-secs): equity must offer sufficient excess return

## Trends he has ridden

- **American consumer brands** (Coke, Gillette → consumer trust)
- **Insurance float** (GEICO, Gen Re) — other people's money to invest
- **Railroads / infrastructure** with oligopoly economics (BNSF)
- **Banks** when understood and conservatively run (Bank of America)
- **India context:** prefers understandable franchises; historically cautious on unpredictable policy-heavy sectors

## How he makes money

| Mechanism | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Compounding** | Berkshire retains earnings; subsidiaries reinvest at high ROIC |
| **Float** | Insurance premiums invested before claims paid |
| **Concentration** | Few huge positions (Apple, Amex, Coca-Cola) |
| **Patience** | Decades; taxes minimized by not selling |
| **Optionality** | Cash in crises to buy distressed quality (2008, 2020) |

## What he avoids

- IPO hype (most offerings overpriced)
- Leverage for investors
- Businesses requiring constant reinvention every few years
- "Turnarounds" unless asset-backed margin of safety (early career)
- Macro market timing — "forecasters make fortune tellers look good"

## Red flags (Buffett lens)

- Accounting aggression, frequent "one-time" charges
- CEO empire building via low-ROIC acquisitions
- Moat eroding (share loss, margin compression without explanation)
- High capex with no ROIC improvement

## NSE checklist (apply to Indian names)

- [ ] Promoter integrity and related-party history
- [ ] 10Y+ view: can ROCE stay high after competition?
- [ ] Is growth ** funded by equity dilution** or internal accruals?
- [ ] Regulatory risk to moat (telecom, mining, tobacco)
